# Paintings for *SQL from Zero to Hero*

A brief for Neha. The cover elephant set the look of the book; these paintings are meant to
sit before each chapter and feel like they belong to the same family. You do not need to
understand the technical side. Each entry says what to paint and, in one line, why that
picture suits the chapter.

## The look to keep

Taken from the cover elephant:

- **Broken colour.** The subject is built from separate dabs and planes of saturated colour
  (yellow, red, teal, violet, deep blue) rather than smooth blending. Up close it is
  abstract; from a step back it is a clear figure.
- **One strong subject per painting.** Large, close, and cropped boldly, the way the elephant
  fills the frame. No busy backgrounds.
- **A calmer ground behind the subject.** The soft, streaky blue behind the elephant lets the
  colourful subject stand out. Use a quiet ground of your choice for each painting.
- **Indian subjects,** drawn from craft, architecture, animals and everyday life. No
  computers, screens, servers or cartoon "tech" imagery anywhere. The book's ideas are
  carried by real things.
- **Dark outlines are used sparingly,** in short strokes, like the tusk lines and the eye.

## Practical specifications

| | |
|---|---|
| Orientation | Portrait. The cover is 2250 × 2775 px, a ratio of about 0.81. Use the same, e.g. **30 × 37 cm** or **45 × 55 cm** |
| Safe area | Keep anything important **at least 1.5 cm from every edge**. The printer trims a little |
| Photographing | Flat daylight from a window, no flash, camera square on to the canvas, no shadows across the surface. Shoot at the highest resolution, a phone is fine. Include a plain white sheet in one test shot so colours can be matched |
| Wet paint | Please let the painting dry fully before photographing, so wet gloss does not show up as glare |
| Order | Do the ★ paintings first (see below). The rest can follow at whatever pace suits |

## Where to start

The full list has one painting for each of the 54 chapters, but a book with 12 openers and
one showpiece is already complete. **Start with the ★ paintings**, one for each of the 12
parts, plus the ★★ chapter 34 painting, which is the one the whole book was written around.
The remaining paintings can be added in later editions.

## Colour by part

Each part gets its own dominant colour, so a reader can feel the book changing as they
move through it. This is a suggestion only; change it if a painting asks for something else.

| Part | Theme | Dominant colours |

## Part I — Foundations

**★ 1. The Relational Model and Why PostgreSQL — A banyan tree with aerial roots.**
A great banyan with roots dropping from its branches and joining the ground. *Things,
connected to other things by roots, is the whole idea of the chapter.*

**2. Setting Up PostgreSQL — A hand lighting a diya.**
Close on a hand and a single flame. *The first step, when nothing is installed yet.*

**3. Databases, Schemas, and the Cluster — A stepwell (baori).**
Looking down a deep stepwell, level below level. *Databases inside a cluster, schemas inside
a database: rooms within rooms.*

## Part II — Core SQL

**★ 4. Creating and Altering Tables — A handloom with warp and weft.**
Close on threads on a loom, forming a grid. *A table is a grid of rows and columns.*

**5. Inserting, Updating, and Deleting Data — A farmer sowing seed.**
A figure scattering seed across a field. *Putting data in, changing it, taking it out.*

**6. Querying Fundamentals — Chinese fishing nets at Kochi.**
The big lifted net against a bright sky. *A query is a net: you choose what it catches.*

**7. Filtering, Operators, and Three-Valued Logic — A covered clay pot (matka).**
A pot with its lid on, contents unseen. *NULL means "unknown". Not true, not false, just
covered.*

**8. Aggregation and Grouping — Mounds of spices in a market.**
Cones of turmeric, chilli and coriander in a row. *Many things gathered into piles and
counted.*

## Part III — Combining Data

**★ 9. Joins — The Sangam, where two rivers meet.**
Two rivers, two colours, flowing together. *That is exactly what a join does.*

**10. Subqueries and EXISTS — A stack of dabbawala tiffin tins.**
A tiered steel tiffin carrier, each tin holding its own meal. *A query inside a query
inside a query.*

**11. Set Operations — Kites overlapping in an Uttarayan sky.**
Two clusters of kites crossing in the sky over Ahmedabad. *What two groups share, and
what only one has.*

**12. CTEs and Recursive Queries — A mandala, pattern inside pattern.**
A round mandala repeating towards the centre. *Recursion: a rule applied to its own
result.*

## Part IV — Data Types and Integrity

**★ 13. Numeric and Character Types — A jeweller's balance.**
A pair of small brass scales weighing gold in Zaveri Bazaar. *Getting the exact amount
right, as with money.*

**14. Temporal Types — The Samrat Yantra sundial at Jantar Mantar.**
The great stone sundial and its long shadow. *Time and time zones.*

**15. Constraints and Referential Integrity — The wall of Kumbhalgarh fort.**
The wall winding along the hills. *Rules that keep bad data out.*

**16. PostgreSQL-Native Types — Kathakali faces.**
Three or four faces in full colour and makeup. *Many special roles, each with its own
character.*

**17. JSONB in Depth — A kantha patchwork quilt.**
Close on hand-stitched cloth pieced from scraps. *Flexible and free-form, useful, but
"a table you refused to model".*

## Part V — Enterprise Database Design

**★ 18. Normalization and Deliberate Denormalization — A woman winnowing rice.**

belongs where.*

**19. Modeling Real Entities — The courtyard of a haveli.**
Rooms around an open courtyard, each with its own purpose. *Each part of the business

**20. Keys and Identity Strategy at Scale — An ornate brass padlock and key.**
A big Rajasthani lock with its key beside it. *Every row's unique identity.*

**21. Structuring a New Enterprise Database — Jaipur from above.**
The planned pink city from above, streets in a neat grid. *A database laid out by design,
not by accident.*

**22. The Table Design Checklist — A mason with a plumb line.**
A bricklayer checking a wall is straight before going higher. *Check before you build.*

**23. The Column Design Checklist — Sorting beads to string a necklace.**

**24. Design Anti-Patterns and Safe Refactoring — Bamboo scaffolding on an old building.**
A heritage facade being repaired behind a lattice of bamboo. *Fixing something old

## How this list works

Each subject was chosen because it *is* the idea in real life, not an illustration of software:
a join is where two rivers meet; NULL is the pot with the lid on. If a different subject
speaks to you more for any chapter, swap it, as long as it is Indian, close-up, and has one
strong subject. Just tell Piyus which chapter it is for.
