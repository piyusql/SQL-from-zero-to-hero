# Chapter 25 Exercises — Window Functions

Four sessions. Each is self-contained and runnable against the sample datasets alone, with
outputs shown from a real PostgreSQL 15.10 run. Sessions 25.1, 25.3 and 25.4 only read
`retail`; 25.2 reads `hr` and builds one scratch database.

## Practice Session 25.1 — Running totals and moving averages

**Goal.** Build a monthly revenue report with a year-to-date total, a moving average that
only reports full windows, a share of the grand total, and a trailing-window figure measured
in calendar days rather than rows.

**Setup.** The small `retail` dataset. Revenue means orders whose `status` is not
`cancelled` or `returned`. Months are calendar months in Indian time.

```sql
\c retail
\pset null '(null)'
SET TIME ZONE 'Asia/Kolkata';
SELECT count(*) AS orders, min(placed_at)::date AS first_day, max(placed_at)::date AS last_day
FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned');
```

**Task.**

1. For every month from January 2024 to December 2024, show `revenue`, `cumulative` (all
   months since the start) and `ytd` (restarting each January). Restrict the output to 2024
   *without changing the numbers*: the 2024 rows must carry the same cumulative values as
   when the whole history is listed. Only the first three months are needed in your answer.
2. Add `avg3`, a three-month moving average that is `NULL` until three months are in the frame
   (use `count(*) OVER w` inside a `CASE`). Show months 1 to 4.
3. Add `pct_of_total`, each month's share of the all-time revenue, to one decimal place, and
   check that the shares sum to about 100.
4. Daily revenue, then `trailing_7d`: revenue over the seven calendar days ending on that
   day, counting days with no orders as zero. Use `RANGE` with an `interval` offset, not
   `ROWS`, and explain what `ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING` would have measured instead. Show
   three consecutive days from early March 2024 (skip the first 60 days of the history).
5. Repeat task 1 with the month filter written in the same query level as the window
   (`WHERE month < '2025-01-01'` next to the `sum(...) OVER`). Explain why the numbers
   change *only if* the filter removes rows from the start of the history, and show it by
   filtering to 2024-11 onward.

**Solution.**

Step 1. The window must see every month, so the filter goes in an outer query. The
`cumulative` column has no `PARTITION BY`; `ytd` partitions by the year.

```sql
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned') GROUP BY 1),
w AS (
  SELECT month, revenue,
         sum(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS cumulative,
         sum(revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY extract(year FROM month) ORDER BY month) AS ytd
  FROM   m)
SELECT * FROM w WHERE month < '2025-01-01' ORDER BY month LIMIT 3;
```

```text
   month    | revenue  | cumulative |    ytd
------------+----------+------------+-----------
 2024-01-01 | 83791.42 |   83791.42 |  83791.42
 2024-02-01 | 78645.91 |  162437.33 | 162437.33
 2024-03-01 | 94637.24 |  257074.57 | 257074.57
(3 rows)
```

Step 2. A named window shared by two functions, and a `CASE` that hides partial frames.

```sql
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned') GROUP BY 1)
SELECT month, revenue,
       CASE WHEN count(*) OVER w = 3 THEN round(avg(revenue) OVER w, 2) END AS avg3
FROM   m WINDOW w AS (ORDER BY month ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)
ORDER  BY month LIMIT 4;
```

```text
   month    | revenue  |   avg3
------------+----------+----------
 2024-01-01 | 83791.42 |   (null)
 2024-02-01 | 78645.91 |   (null)
 2024-03-01 | 94637.24 | 85691.52
 2024-04-01 | 90497.45 | 87926.87
(4 rows)
```

Step 3. `sum(...) OVER ()` is the grand total repeated on every row, computed once.

```sql
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned') GROUP BY 1),
s AS (SELECT month, round(100 * revenue / sum(revenue) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_total FROM m)
SELECT count(*) AS months, sum(pct_of_total) AS shares_sum, max(pct_of_total) AS biggest FROM s;
```

```text
 months | shares_sum | biggest
--------+------------+---------
     33 |      100.4 |     4.2
(1 row)
```

The shares sum to 100.4, not 100: each of the 33 months is rounded to one decimal place, and
the rounding errors add up. Round once, at the end, if a total must reconcile.

Step 4. `RANGE` with an interval offset works on the `date` value, so a calendar gap shrinks
nothing: the frame is defined by distance, not by how many rows exist.

```sql
WITH d AS (
  SELECT placed_at::date AS day, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned') GROUP BY 1)
SELECT day, revenue,
       sum(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY day RANGE BETWEEN interval '6 days' PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) AS trailing_7d,
       sum(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY day ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) AS last_7_rows
FROM   d ORDER BY day OFFSET 60 LIMIT 3;
```

```text
    day     | revenue | trailing_7d | last_7_rows
------------+---------+-------------+-------------
 2024-03-07 | 5697.48 |    16209.97 |    16667.48
 2024-03-08 | 1635.80 |    17436.33 |    17845.77
 2024-03-09 | 2791.08 |    20227.41 |    20227.41
(3 rows)
```

`last_7_rows` adds the previous six *rows*, and since there is one row per day that had
orders, it reaches back further than seven days whenever a day is missing. The two columns
differ on the first two rows and agree on 9 March, where every one of the seven days had
orders. `RANGE` answers "the last seven days". A day with no orders is an absent row that
would have contributed zero, so no calendar table is needed for this figure (you would need
one to *display* the zero days).

Step 5. Now the filter inside the window's query level.

```sql
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned') GROUP BY 1)
SELECT month, revenue, sum(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS cumulative
FROM   m WHERE month >= '2024-11-01' ORDER BY month LIMIT 3;
```

```text
   month    | revenue  | cumulative
------------+----------+------------
 2024-11-01 | 84831.00 |   84831.00
 2024-12-01 | 84120.63 |  168951.63
 2025-01-01 | 69626.59 |  238578.22
(3 rows)
```

The cumulative figure starts again at November because January to October were filtered out
before the window ran. A filter that trims only the *end* of the history (the `month < '2025-01-01'` case) leaves
earlier cumulative values untouched, which is why this bug survives a check of the first
rows.

## Practice Session 25.2 — Top-N per group

**Goal.** Return the three best-paid employees per department three ways, decide what
"top 3" should mean when salaries tie, prove the three methods agree, and see which one
reads fewer buffers for 40 departments of about 70 people.

**Setup.** `hr` is read-only, so the plan comparison runs on a copy in a scratch database.

```bash
dropdb --if-exists ch25_ws2
createdb ch25_ws2
pg_dump -t departments -t employees hr | psql -q -d ch25_ws2
```

```sql
\c ch25_ws2
\pset null '(null)'
SELECT count(*) AS employees, count(DISTINCT dept_id) AS departments,
       count(DISTINCT salary) AS distinct_salaries FROM employees;
```

**Task.**

1. Top 3 by salary per department with `row_number()`, ties broken by `emp_id`. How many rows?
2. Repeat with `rank() <= 3` and `dense_rank() <= 3` and count rows. Explain each count.
3. Which departments contribute more than three rows under `rank`? Show two of them and
   the ranks they return.
4. The single best-paid employee per department with `DISTINCT ON`, then with `row_number`,
   then with `LATERAL ... LIMIT 1`. Prove all three identical with `EXCEPT`.
5. Create the index `(dept_id, salary DESC, emp_id)`. Compare the top-3 window query and
   the top-3 `LATERAL` query with `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS, COSTS OFF, TIMING OFF, SUMMARY OFF)`,
   after `SET max_parallel_workers_per_gather = 0`. Report the plan node types and the
   total buffers (hit plus read) for each, and say which wins here and why that differs from
   the 200,000-row example in the chapter.
6. Drop the index and the scratch database.

**Solution.**

Step 1. The tiebreaker makes the result deterministic.

```sql
SELECT count(*) AS rows_returned
FROM  (SELECT row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY dept_id ORDER BY salary DESC, emp_id) AS rn
       FROM employees) s WHERE rn <= 3;
```

```text
 rows_returned
---------------
           120
(1 row)
```

Step 2. Ties at the boundary add rows.

```sql
SELECT (SELECT count(*) FROM (SELECT rank() OVER (PARTITION BY dept_id ORDER BY salary DESC) AS r
                              FROM employees) s WHERE r <= 3) AS rank_top3,
       (SELECT count(*) FROM (SELECT dense_rank() OVER (PARTITION BY dept_id ORDER BY salary DESC) AS r
                              FROM employees) s WHERE r <= 3) AS dense_rank_top3;
```

```text
 rank_top3 | dense_rank_top3
-----------+-----------------
       124 |             129
(1 row)
```

`row_number` returns exactly 3 per department (40 x 3 = 120). `rank <= 3` returned 124: everyone
whose *rank* is at most 3, so ties near the boundary bring extra rows. `dense_rank <= 3`
returned 129: the top three distinct salary levels. Neither is wrong, but they answer different
questions from "three employees".

Step 3. Departments where `rank` returns more than three:

```sql
SELECT dept_id, count(*) AS rows_under_rank, array_agg(DISTINCT r ORDER BY r) AS ranks_returned
FROM  (SELECT dept_id, salary, rank() OVER (PARTITION BY dept_id ORDER BY salary DESC) AS r
       FROM employees) s
WHERE r <= 3 GROUP BY dept_id HAVING count(*) > 3 ORDER BY dept_id LIMIT 2;
```

```text
 dept_id | rows_under_rank | ranks_returned
---------+-----------------+----------------
      21 |               4 | {1,2}
      29 |               4 | {1,2,3}
(2 rows)
```

Step 4. Three ways to keep one row per department, and a proof they agree once the
tiebreaker is the same.

```sql
WITH a AS (SELECT DISTINCT ON (dept_id) dept_id, emp_id FROM employees
           ORDER BY dept_id, salary DESC, emp_id),
b AS (SELECT dept_id, emp_id FROM
        (SELECT dept_id, emp_id,
                row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY dept_id ORDER BY salary DESC, emp_id) AS rn
         FROM employees) s WHERE rn = 1),
c AS (SELECT d.dept_id, e.emp_id FROM departments d
      CROSS JOIN LATERAL (SELECT emp_id FROM employees WHERE dept_id = d.dept_id
                          ORDER BY salary DESC, emp_id LIMIT 1) e)
SELECT (SELECT count(*) FROM a) AS rows_a,
       (SELECT count(*) FROM (SELECT * FROM a EXCEPT SELECT * FROM b) x) AS a_not_b,
       (SELECT count(*) FROM (SELECT * FROM b EXCEPT SELECT * FROM a) x) AS b_not_a,
       (SELECT count(*) FROM (SELECT * FROM c EXCEPT SELECT * FROM a) x) AS c_not_a,
       (SELECT count(*) FROM (SELECT * FROM a EXCEPT SELECT * FROM c) x) AS a_not_c;
```

```text
 rows_a | a_not_b | b_not_a | c_not_a | a_not_c
--------+---------+---------+---------+---------
     40 |       0 |       0 |       0 |       0
(1 row)
```

Step 5. Create the index, warm it, and compare.

```sql
CREATE INDEX emp_dept_salary ON employees (dept_id, salary DESC, emp_id);
VACUUM ANALYZE employees;
SET max_parallel_workers_per_gather = 0;
SET enable_seqscan = off;
SELECT count(*) FROM employees WHERE dept_id > 0;
SELECT count(*) FROM departments d, LATERAL (SELECT 1 FROM employees WHERE dept_id = d.dept_id
                                             ORDER BY salary DESC, emp_id LIMIT 3) e;
RESET enable_seqscan;
```

```sql
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS, COSTS OFF, TIMING OFF, SUMMARY OFF)
SELECT emp_id FROM (SELECT emp_id, row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY dept_id ORDER BY salary DESC, emp_id) AS rn
                    FROM employees) s WHERE rn <= 3;
```

```text
                                        QUERY PLAN
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Subquery Scan on s (actual rows=120 loops=1)
   Buffers: shared hit=16
   ->  WindowAgg (actual rows=120 loops=1)
         Run Condition: (row_number() OVER (?) <= 3)
         Buffers: shared hit=16
         ->  Index Only Scan using emp_dept_salary on employees (actual rows=2774 loops=1)
               Heap Fetches: 0
               Buffers: shared hit=16
```

```sql
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS, COSTS OFF, TIMING OFF, SUMMARY OFF)
SELECT e.emp_id FROM departments d
CROSS JOIN LATERAL (SELECT emp_id FROM employees WHERE dept_id = d.dept_id
                    ORDER BY salary DESC, emp_id LIMIT 3) e;
```

```text
                                       QUERY PLAN
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Nested Loop (actual rows=120 loops=1)
   Buffers: shared hit=83
   ->  Seq Scan on departments d (actual rows=40 loops=1)
         Buffers: shared hit=1
   ->  Limit (actual rows=3 loops=40)
         Buffers: shared hit=82
         ->  Index Only Scan using emp_dept_salary on employees (actual rows=3 loops=40)
               Index Cond: (dept_id = d.dept_id)
               Heap Fetches: 0
               Buffers: shared hit=82
```

With 40 groups of about 70 rows, the window reads the whole (small) index in a single
pass, while `LATERAL` pays a descent from the root for each of the 40 departments. When groups
are this small the descents cost more than the scan they avoid, the reverse of the chapter's
50 groups of 4,000 rows. The rule is that `LATERAL` wins when the rows *skipped* per group are
many; measure with your own group sizes.

Step 6. Clean up.

```bash
dropdb ch25_ws2
```

## Practice Session 25.3 — Period-over-period with LAG

**Goal.** Compute month-over-month and year-over-year change, survive a missing month,
measure the gap between a customer's consecutive orders, and carry a last-known value
forward without `IGNORE NULLS`.

**Setup.** The small `retail` dataset, months in Indian time.

```sql
\c retail
\pset null '(null)'
SET TIME ZONE 'Asia/Kolkata';
SELECT count(*) AS orders, count(DISTINCT customer_id) AS customers FROM orders;
```

**Task.**

1. For each month of 2025, show revenue (excluding `cancelled` and `returned`), the previous
   month's revenue, month-over-month percentage change, and the same month a year earlier.
   Show January to March.
2. Delete nothing, but simulate a missing month by excluding April 2025 in the `WHERE` of
   the base query. Show what `lag` reports for May and June, then repair it with a
   `generate_series` calendar so May's previous value is `NULL`.
3. For customer 1, list each order with `days_since_prev` and `days_until_next` using
   `lag` and `lead` over `placed_at`.
4. How many customers with at least two orders have ever gone more than 365 days between
   consecutive orders? (Count customers, not gaps.)
5. `lag(shipped_at) IGNORE NULLS` does not exist. For customer 6, produce
   `last_known_shipped`: the most recent non-`NULL` `shipped_at` at or before each order.
   Hint: `count(shipped_at) OVER (ORDER BY placed_at)` numbers the non-null values.

**Solution.**

Step 1. Filter outside the windows, since `lag` must see the earlier rows.

```sql
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned') GROUP BY 1),
w AS (
  SELECT month, revenue,
         lag(revenue)     OVER (ORDER BY month) AS prev,
         lag(revenue, 12) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS year_ago
  FROM   m)
SELECT month, revenue, prev, round(100 * (revenue - prev) / prev, 1) AS mom_pct, year_ago
FROM   w WHERE month >= '2025-01-01' ORDER BY month LIMIT 3;
```

```text
   month    |  revenue  |   prev   | mom_pct | year_ago
------------+-----------+----------+---------+----------
 2025-01-01 |  69626.59 | 84120.63 |   -17.2 | 83791.42
 2025-02-01 |  85487.75 | 69626.59 |    22.8 | 78645.91
 2025-03-01 | 102123.30 | 85487.75 |    19.5 | 94637.24
(3 rows)
```

Step 2. First the naive version, then the calendar.

```sql
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned')
         AND date_trunc('month', placed_at) <> '2025-04-01'::timestamptz GROUP BY 1),
w AS (SELECT month, lag(month) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS prev_month,
             lag(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS prev_revenue FROM m)
SELECT * FROM w WHERE month BETWEEN '2025-03-01' AND '2025-06-01' ORDER BY month;
```

```text
   month    | prev_month | prev_revenue
------------+------------+--------------
 2025-03-01 | 2025-02-01 |     85487.75
 2025-05-01 | 2025-03-01 |    102123.30
 2025-06-01 | 2025-05-01 |     72697.30
(3 rows)
```

```sql
WITH m AS (
  SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
  FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned')
         AND date_trunc('month', placed_at) <> '2025-04-01'::timestamptz GROUP BY 1),
cal AS (SELECT d::date AS month FROM generate_series('2024-01-01', '2026-08-01', interval '1 month') d),
w AS (SELECT c.month, m.revenue, lag(m.revenue) OVER (ORDER BY c.month) AS prev_revenue
      FROM cal c LEFT JOIN m USING (month))
SELECT * FROM w WHERE month BETWEEN '2025-03-01' AND '2025-06-01' ORDER BY month;
```

```text
   month    |  revenue  | prev_revenue
------------+-----------+--------------
 2025-03-01 | 102123.30 |     85487.75
 2025-04-01 |    (null) |    102123.30
 2025-05-01 |  72697.30 |       (null)
 2025-06-01 |  67582.99 |     72697.30
(4 rows)
```

In the naive version May's "previous month" is March. In the repaired one April exists as a
row with `NULL` revenue, so May's previous value is `NULL`, which is true: the report does
not know it.

Step 3. `lag` looks back, `lead` looks forward; both return `NULL` off the ends.

```sql
SELECT id, placed_at::date AS placed,
       placed_at::date - lag(placed_at::date)  OVER w AS days_since_prev,
       lead(placed_at::date) OVER w - placed_at::date AS days_until_next
FROM   orders WHERE customer_id = 1 WINDOW w AS (ORDER BY placed_at) ORDER BY placed_at;
```

```text
  id  |   placed   | days_since_prev | days_until_next
------+------------+-----------------+-----------------
  383 | 2024-05-14 |          (null) |              77
 2386 | 2024-07-30 |              77 |              13
  806 | 2024-08-12 |              13 |             159
 2462 | 2025-01-18 |             159 |          (null)
(4 rows)
```

Step 4. The per-customer window is `PARTITION BY customer_id`; the outer query aggregates it.

```sql
WITH g AS (
  SELECT customer_id,
         placed_at::date - lag(placed_at::date) OVER (PARTITION BY customer_id ORDER BY placed_at) AS gap
  FROM   orders)
SELECT count(DISTINCT customer_id) AS customers_with_long_gap FROM g WHERE gap > 365;
```

```text
 customers_with_long_gap
-------------------------
                     272
(1 row)
```

Step 5. `count(shipped_at)` increments only on non-null rows, so it is the index of the latest
non-null value; `array_agg ... FILTER` collects them in order and the count picks the last.

```sql
SELECT id, placed_at::date AS placed, shipped_at::date AS shipped,
       (array_agg(shipped_at::date) FILTER (WHERE shipped_at IS NOT NULL)
          OVER (ORDER BY placed_at))[count(shipped_at) OVER (ORDER BY placed_at)] AS last_known_shipped
FROM   orders WHERE customer_id = 6 ORDER BY placed_at;
```

```text
  id  |   placed   |  shipped   | last_known_shipped
------+------------+------------+--------------------
 2133 | 2024-02-01 | (null)     | (null)
  346 | 2025-02-01 | 2025-02-05 | 2025-02-05
 1306 | 2025-12-03 | 2025-12-06 | 2025-12-06
  960 | 2026-05-17 | (null)     | 2025-12-06
(4 rows)
```

Before the first shipment the subscript is 0, which yields `NULL`, the right answer. The
array grows with each row. For one customer's handful of orders that is fine; I have not
measured it on large partitions, where a `LEFT JOIN LATERAL` for the latest earlier non-null
row is the alternative I would test first.

## Practice Session 25.4 — A ROWS vs RANGE discrepancy

**Goal.** Reproduce a finance reconciliation where a running-total report and its audit
disagree only on some rows, find out why, and fix it.

**Setup.** The small `retail` dataset. Finance reports a per-order running revenue total in
date order. The dashboard query orders by the date only; the audit script orders by date and
`id` and asks for `ROWS`.

```sql
\c retail
\pset null '(null)'
SET TIME ZONE 'Asia/Kolkata';
CREATE TEMP VIEW dash AS
SELECT id, placed_at::date AS day, total_amount,
       sum(total_amount) OVER (ORDER BY placed_at::date) AS running
FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned');
CREATE TEMP VIEW audit AS
SELECT id, placed_at::date AS day, total_amount,
       sum(total_amount) OVER (ORDER BY placed_at::date, id ROWS UNBOUNDED PRECEDING) AS running
FROM   orders WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned');
SELECT count(*) AS orders FROM dash;
```

**Task.**

1. Count the orders whose `running` value differs between the two views.
2. Show the rows for the first day on which the two disagree.
3. Prove the disagreement disappears on the *last* order of each day: count the last-of-day
   rows where they differ.
4. Explain, from the frame definitions, why the totals differ within a day. State which report
   is correct for "sold by this order" and which for "sold by end of this day".
5. Show that `ROWS` without the `id` tiebreaker is unstable: for the day found in step 2,
   compute the running total under `ORDER BY day, id` and `ORDER BY day, id DESC`, and
   show that the same order gets different values.
6. Use `EXCLUDE CURRENT ROW` with `RANGE ... CURRENT ROW` to give each order the average
   amount of the *other* orders that day. Which orders get `NULL`, and why?
7. Use `GROUPS BETWEEN 1 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW` to give each order the count of orders
   on its own day and the previous *active* day, and compare with `RANGE '1 day'` for
   the same rows on the first day that follows a gap.

**Solution.**

Step 1. Join the views by `id`.

```sql
SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE d.running <> a.running) AS differing,
       count(*) AS total
FROM   dash d JOIN audit a USING (id);
```

```text
 differing | total
-----------+-------
      1116 |  1974
(1 row)
```

Step 2. The first day with a disagreement, by date.

```sql
WITH bad AS (
  SELECT min(d.day) AS day FROM dash d JOIN audit a USING (id) WHERE d.running <> a.running)
SELECT d.id, d.day, d.total_amount, d.running AS dash_running, a.running AS audit_running
FROM   dash d JOIN audit a USING (id) JOIN bad ON bad.day = d.day ORDER BY a.running;
```

```text
  id  |    day     | total_amount | dash_running | audit_running
------+------------+--------------+--------------+---------------
 1289 | 2024-01-01 |       198.76 |      2424.75 |        198.76
 1776 | 2024-01-01 |      2225.99 |      2424.75 |       2424.75
(2 rows)
```

Step 3. The last order of each day is the one with the highest `id` in the audit ordering.

```sql
WITH j AS (
  SELECT d.id, d.day, d.running AS dr, a.running AS ar,
         row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY d.day ORDER BY d.id DESC) AS from_end
  FROM   dash d JOIN audit a USING (id))
SELECT count(*) AS last_of_day_rows, count(*) FILTER (WHERE dr <> ar) AS differ
FROM   j WHERE from_end = 1;
```

```text
 last_of_day_rows | differ
------------------+--------
              858 |      0
(1 row)
```

Step 4. The default frame is `RANGE BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW`, and in
`RANGE` mode "current row" means the current row *and all its peers*: rows with the same
`ORDER BY` value. On a day with three orders, all three see the day's full total. The audit's
`ROWS` frame stops at the row itself, so each order sees only the orders before it in the
`(day, id)` order. Both are correct answers to different questions. The dashboard says "sold
by the end of this day", stamped on every order; the audit says "sold by this order". They
must agree on the last order of the day, and step 3 confirms they do. They only ever
disagree on a row that has a same-day peer after it.

Step 5. Two different tiebreakers, same day.

```sql
WITH bad AS (
  SELECT min(d.day) AS day FROM dash d JOIN audit a USING (id) WHERE d.running <> a.running),
o AS (SELECT id, placed_at::date AS day, total_amount FROM orders
      WHERE status NOT IN ('cancelled','returned'))
SELECT id, total_amount,
       sum(total_amount) OVER (ORDER BY day, id      ROWS UNBOUNDED PRECEDING) AS by_id_asc,
       sum(total_amount) OVER (ORDER BY day, id DESC ROWS UNBOUNDED PRECEDING) AS by_id_desc
FROM   o WHERE day <= (SELECT day FROM bad) ORDER BY id;
```

```text
  id  | total_amount | by_id_asc | by_id_desc
------+--------------+-----------+------------
 1289 |       198.76 |    198.76 |    2424.75
 1776 |      2225.99 |   2424.75 |    2225.99
(2 rows)
```

Read only the rows for the day found in step 2 (earlier days appear too, since the window
needs them): the same order carries a different running value under each tiebreaker. With
*no* tiebreaker the partial sums are whichever order the executor happened to produce, which
can change with the plan. A `ROWS` running total needs a unique `ORDER BY`.

Step 6. `EXCLUDE CURRENT ROW` removes the current row from its own frame. The frame here is the
current row's peers, so what remains is the *other* orders on the same day.

```sql
SELECT id, placed_at::date AS day, total_amount,
       round(avg(total_amount) OVER (ORDER BY placed_at::date
             RANGE BETWEEN CURRENT ROW AND CURRENT ROW EXCLUDE CURRENT ROW), 2) AS avg_of_others
FROM   orders WHERE placed_at >= '2024-01-04' AND placed_at < '2024-01-08' ORDER BY day, id;
```

```text
  id  |    day     | total_amount | avg_of_others
------+------------+--------------+---------------
  970 | 2024-01-04 |      1032.80 |        512.42
 1892 | 2024-01-04 |       816.39 |        620.62
 2324 | 2024-01-04 |       208.44 |        924.60
 2198 | 2024-01-06 |      1503.30 |        (null)
  663 | 2024-01-07 |       335.58 |       1715.47
 1242 | 2024-01-07 |      1715.47 |        335.58
(6 rows)
```

The single order on 6 January has no peers left once it is excluded, so its frame is empty and
`avg` of nothing is `NULL`.

Step 7. `GROUPS` counts peer groups, `RANGE '1 day'` counts calendar distance. Order 2198 is the
first on 6 January, after a day with no orders.

```sql
SELECT id, placed_at::date AS day,
       count(*) OVER (ORDER BY placed_at::date GROUPS BETWEEN 1 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) AS groups_1,
       count(*) OVER (ORDER BY placed_at::date RANGE BETWEEN interval '1 day' PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) AS range_1day
FROM   orders WHERE placed_at >= '2024-01-04' AND placed_at < '2024-01-08' ORDER BY day, id;
```

```text
  id  |    day     | groups_1 | range_1day
------+------------+----------+------------
  970 | 2024-01-04 |        3 |          3
 1892 | 2024-01-04 |        3 |          3
 2324 | 2024-01-04 |        3 |          3
 2198 | 2024-01-06 |        4 |          1
  663 | 2024-01-07 |        3 |          3
 1242 | 2024-01-07 |        3 |          3
(6 rows)
```

For order 2198, `GROUPS` reaches back across the empty 5th to take the three orders from the
4th; `RANGE '1 day'` reaches back one calendar day, finds nothing, and counts only the order
itself.
