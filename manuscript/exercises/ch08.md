# Chapter 8 Exercises — Aggregation and Grouping

## Practice Session 8.1 — Sales rollups by dimension

**Goal.** Produce revenue rollups across three dimensions, and decide correctly between
`count(*)` and `count(column)` where the two legitimately disagree.

**Setup.** The small `retail` dataset from Practice Session 2.3.

```bash
psql -d retail
```

Confirm you have the small variant:

```sql
SELECT count(*) FROM order_items;    -- expect 6250
```

Two of the three steps below need a customer attribute (`loyalty_tier`, `city`) alongside
an order amount, and those live in different tables, so they use a single inner join of the
kind Chapter 1 showed you. Joins are Chapter 9's subject and you do not need to understand
the mechanics yet; read `JOIN customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id` as *attach each order to
its customer's row*. Everything being taught here is in the `GROUP BY` and the aggregates.

**Task.**

1. Revenue and order count per calendar month, for 2026 only, oldest month first.
2. Per loyalty tier, report revenue, the number of orders, the number that have actually
   shipped, the percentage shipped, and the mean time from order to shipment. Customers
   with no tier must appear as their own group and must be labelled. Then answer, in
   writing: is the mean delay computed over the right denominator, and how do you know?
3. Revenue and order count per city, top eight by revenue. Then explain what the top row of
   that result actually is, and whether it belongs there.

**Solution.**

**1 — by month.**

```sql
SELECT date_trunc('month', placed_at)::date AS month,
       count(*)                             AS orders,
       sum(total_amount)                    AS revenue
FROM   orders
WHERE  placed_at >= DATE '2026-01-01'
GROUP  BY month
ORDER  BY month;
```

```text
   month    | orders |  revenue
------------+--------+-----------
 2026-01-01 |     88 | 132628.89
 2026-02-01 |     76 | 121545.29
 2026-03-01 |     83 | 123741.04
 2026-04-01 |     91 | 147751.00
 2026-05-01 |     84 | 128501.17
 2026-06-01 |     79 | 117088.10
 2026-07-01 |     88 | 117378.53
 2026-08-01 |     80 | 104594.21
 2026-09-01 |      3 |   3098.04
(9 rows)

```

Two things to take from this. The filter is on `placed_at`, not on the truncated month: that
matters enormously once there is an index on `placed_at`, because `WHERE placed_at >= ...`
can use it and `WHERE date_trunc('month', placed_at) >= ...` cannot without an expression
index. *Filter on the column, group on the expression.*

And September is not a collapse in demand. It is a partial month — the dataset ends on
1 September. Every time-bucketed report has a ragged final bucket, and somebody always reads
it as a trend.

**2 — by loyalty tier, and where `count(*)` and `count(col)` disagree.**

```sql
SELECT coalesce(c.loyalty_tier, 'unenrolled')           AS tier,
       count(*)                                         AS orders,
       sum(o.total_amount)                              AS revenue,
       count(o.shipped_at)                              AS shipped,
       round(100.0 * count(o.shipped_at) / count(*), 1) AS pct_shipped,
       avg(o.shipped_at - o.placed_at)                  AS avg_delay
FROM   orders o
JOIN   customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
GROUP  BY c.loyalty_tier
ORDER  BY revenue DESC;
```

```text
    tier    | orders |  revenue   | shipped | pct_shipped |       avg_delay
------------+--------+------------+---------+-------------+-----------------------
 unenrolled |    994 | 1443220.88 |     686 |        69.0 | 1 day 22:55:21.865889
 silver     |    557 |  849477.49 |     390 |        70.0 | 1 day 21:18:55.384615
 bronze     |    512 |  741014.47 |     350 |        68.4 | 1 day 25:15:56.571428
 gold       |    437 |  645269.77 |     307 |        70.3 | 1 day 24:50:01.954397
(4 rows)

```

You can `GROUP BY c.loyalty_tier` while selecting `coalesce(c.loyalty_tier, 'unenrolled')`:
the select-list rule is satisfied because the expression is built from the grouping key.
Grouping by the `coalesce` itself would also run, and would be wrong in general, because it
merges genuine NULLs with any real row whose tier happened to be the string `'unenrolled'`.

The largest revenue bucket is the one with no tier at all: 994 orders, 39% of revenue,
invisible in any report that filtered
`WHERE loyalty_tier IN ('bronze','silver','gold')`. That filter would not error. It would
quietly produce a loyalty programme that appears to cover the whole business.

Now the two counts. `count(*)` and `count(o.shipped_at)` differ in every row, and both are
right, because they answer different questions. `count(*)` is *orders placed*.
`count(o.shipped_at)` is *orders with a known shipping date*, which in this schema means
*orders shipped*. The percentage is only meaningful because the numerator and denominator
were chosen deliberately. Use `count(o.shipped_at)` on both sides of the division — an easy
slip when you are copying the expression — and every tier reports a fulfilment rate of 100%.
That number would ship to production looking entirely reasonable.

> **Trap —** `100.0 *` is load-bearing. `count(...)` returns `bigint`, and
> `count(a) / count(b)` is integer division, which truncates 0.684 to 0. Multiplying by a
> `numeric` literal first promotes the whole expression. This is the single most common
> arithmetic bug in aggregate reporting and it presents as a column of zeroes.

**Is `avg_delay` computed over the right denominator?**

Yes — and you can prove it from the numbers on the page. `avg` ignores NULLs, so it divided
by 350 for bronze, not 512. The 162 unshipped bronze orders have a NULL `shipped_at`, so
`o.shipped_at - o.placed_at` is NULL for them, so they were excluded entirely.

That is the answer you want, because those orders **have no delay yet**. Including them as
zero would claim they shipped instantly and would drag the average down. Including them as
"today minus `placed_at`" would be a different and also defensible metric — *age of open
orders* — but it is not the one this query computes, and it would need
`coalesce(shipped_at, now())` spelled out.

The general test, which is worth writing on a sticky note: **for each nullable column in an
aggregate, state in one sentence what its NULL means. If the sentence is "the value does not
exist", the default behaviour is right. If the sentence is "the value is zero", you need
`coalesce`. If you cannot write the sentence, stop and go read the schema.**

**3 — by city.**

```sql
SELECT coalesce(c.city, '(unknown)') AS city,
       count(*)                      AS orders,
       sum(o.total_amount)           AS revenue
FROM   orders o
JOIN   customers c ON c.id = o.customer_id
GROUP  BY c.city
ORDER  BY revenue DESC
LIMIT  8;
```

```text
     city      | orders |  revenue
---------------+--------+-----------
 (unknown)     |    307 | 465684.20
 Bhubaneswar   |    154 | 242128.87
 Hyderabad     |    136 | 204241.20
 Nagpur        |    133 | 191715.08
 Kochi         |    126 | 185669.59
 Visakhapatnam |    116 | 175196.66
 Bengaluru     |    115 | 172605.68
 Chandigarh    |    115 | 171494.56
(8 rows)

```

`LIMIT` is applied after grouping and ordering, so it takes the top eight *groups*, not the
first eight rows of the table. That is the clause order from Chapter 6 doing its job:
`FROM` → `WHERE` → `GROUP BY` → `HAVING` → `SELECT` → `ORDER BY` → `LIMIT`.

Now the question the task asked about the top row. `(unknown)` is not a city. It is the 128
customers with no city on file, collapsed into one group by the `IS NOT DISTINCT FROM` rule,
and it outranks every real city. That is the correct result and it is also a warning: had
the label not been supplied, this row would render as a blank cell at the top of a chart
titled "Top cities by revenue", and somebody would eventually build a regional plan around
it.

Whether to keep it is a reporting decision, not an SQL one. Keep it and the total reconciles
with step 1; drop it with `WHERE c.city IS NOT NULL` and the column adds up to less than the
revenue you actually earned. Choose deliberately, and if you drop it, say so on the chart.

Grouping by `c.country` instead is the same query with one word changed, and on this dataset
it is a much duller chart — one country dominates and the rest share a thin tail.
Cardinality is what makes a dimension worth plotting, and `count(DISTINCT ...)` on the
candidate column tells you before you build the panel.

## Practice Session 8.2 — `WHERE` versus `HAVING`, measured

**Goal.** Measure what `HAVING` placement costs, find out that PostgreSQL rewrites the easy
case for you, and find the case it will not rewrite.

**Setup.** This session needs the large `retail` variant — about 2,000,000 orders — because
at 2,500 rows every plan finishes in under a millisecond and the difference is noise.

`load.sh` builds a database named after the dataset, so `./load.sh retail lg` would replace
your small `retail` and break the other sessions in Part II. Build the large one beside it,
using the same two files the script uses:

```bash
cd path/to/sql-from-my-heart/datasets

createdb retail_lg
psql -d retail_lg -v ON_ERROR_STOP=1 -f retail/schema.sql
psql -d retail_lg -v ON_ERROR_STOP=1 -f retail/generate-lg.sql
```

About a minute, and 749 MB. Then:

```bash
psql -d retail_lg
```

```sql
SELECT count(*) FROM orders;                     -- 2000000
SELECT count(DISTINCT customer_id) FROM orders;  -- ~255000, varies: lg uses random()
SHOW work_mem;                                   -- 4MB on a default install
```

Your absolute timings will differ from the ones below. The ratios are the point, and they
hold. Run each query two or three times before believing a number, so the table is in cache
and you are measuring CPU rather than your disk.

**Task.**

1. Write a query giving order count and revenue per customer for customers 1–1000, with the
   restriction in `WHERE`. Get `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, COSTS OFF, TIMING OFF)` for it.
2. Write the same query with the restriction moved to `HAVING`. Compare the two plans line
   by line before comparing the timings. Where is `Filter:` attached in each?
3. Now express the same restriction as `HAVING max(customer_id) <= 1000`. Convince yourself
   it returns the same 1,000 rows, then explain the plan.
4. From step 3's plan, work out how much data hit the disk and why.

**Solution.**

**1 — the restriction in `WHERE`.**

```sql
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, COSTS OFF, TIMING OFF)
SELECT customer_id, count(*) AS orders, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
FROM   orders
WHERE  customer_id <= 1000
GROUP  BY customer_id;
```

```text
 Finalize GroupAggregate (actual rows=1000 loops=1)
   Group Key: customer_id
   ->  Gather Merge (actual rows=2715 loops=1)
         Workers Planned: 2
         Workers Launched: 2
         ->  Partial GroupAggregate (actual rows=905 loops=3)
               Group Key: customer_id
               ->  Sort (actual rows=2585 loops=3)
                     Sort Key: customer_id
                     Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 148kB
                     Worker 0:  Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 217kB
                     Worker 1:  Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 301kB
                     ->  Parallel Seq Scan on orders (actual rows=2585 loops=3)
                           Filter: (customer_id <= 1000)
                           Rows Removed by Filter: 664082
 Planning Time: 0.178 ms
 Execution Time: 58.382 ms
```

**2 — the restriction in `HAVING`.**

```sql
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, COSTS OFF, TIMING OFF)
SELECT customer_id, count(*) AS orders, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
FROM   orders
GROUP  BY customer_id
HAVING customer_id <= 1000;
```

```text
 Finalize GroupAggregate (actual rows=1000 loops=1)
   Group Key: customer_id
   ->  Gather Merge (actual rows=2661 loops=1)
         Workers Planned: 2
         Workers Launched: 2
         ->  Partial GroupAggregate (actual rows=887 loops=3)
               Group Key: customer_id
               ->  Sort (actual rows=2585 loops=3)
                     Sort Key: customer_id
                     Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 318kB
                     Worker 0:  Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 134kB
                     Worker 1:  Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 214kB
                     ->  Parallel Seq Scan on orders (actual rows=2585 loops=3)
                           Filter: (customer_id <= 1000)
                           Rows Removed by Filter: 664082
 Planning Time: 0.036 ms
 Execution Time: 48.384 ms
```

The same plan. `Filter: (customer_id <= 1000)` is attached to the `Parallel Seq Scan` in
both, and there is no filter on the aggregate node in either. 58.382 ms against 48.384 ms on
the machine this book was written on — a gap that reverses if you run them in the other
order.

The planner moved the `HAVING` conjunct into `WHERE` before planning began. It may do this
whenever the conjunct contains no aggregate and the query has an ordinary `GROUP BY`,
because a group with no rows cannot exist, so removing rows early cannot remove a group that
should have survived.

If you expected `HAVING` to be slower here — as I did, and as almost every SQL course
teaches — this is the useful kind of being wrong. The lesson is not "`HAVING` is fine". It is
that a performance belief you have not measured on your own version is a rumour.

**3 — the restriction wrapped in an aggregate.**

It returns the same 1,000 rows, because `customer_id` is the grouping key, so within each
group `max(customer_id)` is just `customer_id`.

```sql
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, COSTS OFF, TIMING OFF)
SELECT customer_id, count(*) AS orders, sum(total_amount) AS revenue
FROM   orders
GROUP  BY customer_id
HAVING max(customer_id) <= 1000;
```

```text
 Finalize GroupAggregate (actual rows=1000 loops=1)
   Group Key: customer_id
   Filter: (max(customer_id) <= 1000)
   Rows Removed by Filter: 253896
   ->  Gather Merge (actual rows=672830 loops=1)
         Workers Planned: 2
         Workers Launched: 2
         ->  Sort (actual rows=224277 loops=3)
               Sort Key: customer_id
               Sort Method: external merge  Disk: 23592kB
               Worker 0:  Sort Method: external merge  Disk: 23632kB
               Worker 1:  Sort Method: external merge  Disk: 17432kB
               ->  Partial HashAggregate (actual rows=224277 loops=3)
                     Group Key: customer_id
                     Batches: 17  Memory Usage: 8337kB  Disk Usage: 31208kB
                     Worker 0:  Batches: 17  Memory Usage: 8337kB  Disk Usage: 31208kB
                     Worker 1:  Batches: 17  Memory Usage: 8337kB  Disk Usage: 14928kB
                     ->  Parallel Seq Scan on orders (actual rows=666667 loops=3)
 Planning Time: 0.042 ms
 Execution Time: 496.314 ms
```

496.314 ms against 58.382 ms — eight and a half times slower on this run, and eight to
twelve times across repeated runs — for a query that returns identical rows. The whole cause
is that `max()` made the conjunct un-pushable, so the filter stayed on the aggregate node.

Read the plan bottom-up and the story is unambiguous:

- `Parallel Seq Scan on orders (actual rows=666667 loops=3)` — **no `Filter:` line**. Two
  million rows pass upward, against 7,755 in step 1.
- `Partial HashAggregate ... Group Key: customer_id` — a hash table with roughly 224,000
  entries per process, instead of 1,000.
- `Filter: (max(customer_id) <= 1000)` with `Rows Removed by Filter: 253896` — a quarter of
  a million groups were fully aggregated and then discarded.

**4 — what hit the disk.**

Two spills, both visible in the plan:

- `Batches: 17  Memory Usage: 8337kB  Disk Usage: 31208kB` on the `Partial HashAggregate`.
  The hash table did not fit in `work_mem`, so PostgreSQL partitioned it into 17 batches and
  wrote to temporary files: 31208 kB, 31208 kB and 14928 kB across the leader and its two
  workers, about 76 MB.
- `Sort Method: external merge  Disk: 23592kB` on the `Sort` above it — 23592 kB, 23632 kB
  and 17432 kB, about 63 MB, because the `Gather Merge` needs sorted input and 224,000 rows
  per process do not fit either.

Around 139 MB of temporary files written and read back, to return 1,000 rows totalling a
few kilobytes. That is what "grouped first, filtered afterwards" costs when the planner is
not allowed to help you.

> **Trap —** `Sort Method: external merge` and `Disk Usage:` in a plan mean the query ran out
> of `work_mem` and went to disk. On PostgreSQL 13 and later, hash aggregation spills rather
> than refusing to be chosen, which is a large improvement over the alternative plan but
> makes the overrun *silent* — before 13 the planner simply avoided hashing. Treat any
> `Disk Usage:` in a plan as a finding. Chapter 34 reads these plans line by line.

Raising `work_mem` would remove the spills and some of the gap. It would not remove the real
waste — aggregating 254,896 groups to keep 1,000 — and raising `work_mem` globally to fix one
query is how clusters run out of memory under concurrency, since the setting is per sort or
hash node per backend, not per query. Chapter 45, *Configuration and Resource Tuning*, covers
sizing it properly.

Leave `retail_lg` in place. Chapters 9, 33 and 34 will want it.
